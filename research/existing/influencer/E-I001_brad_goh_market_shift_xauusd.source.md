In this video, professional scalper Fabio Valentini breaks down a Nasdaq scalping and intraday order-flow strategy developed over six months, combining value area migration, cumulative/candle delta, passive absorption, and his proprietary "Nasdaq Effort" model (0:16).
Fabio—who is introduced as a podium finisher in all four quarters of the Robbins World Cup futures challenge and an alpha researcher for seven-figure corporate entities—walks host Brandon through the entire framework live on the chart, explaining how he reads market control, enters trades, manages trailing stops, scales positions, and handles the inevitable drawbacks of trend-following systems (0:09).

1. Core Philosophy & The Three Pillars of the Model

Fabio’s overarching goal is to simplify identifying when one side of the market (buyers or sellers) is genuinely dominating (0:16). Rather than relying on standard price action alone, his system layers three distinct order-flow concepts (0:40):
1. Value Area Migration (Direction): Tracks where "fair value" is shifting from candle to candle or session to session to determine the main intraday drift (0:40).
2. Delta & Absorption (Confirmation): Uses volume delta (aggressive market buyers vs. aggressive market sellers) and passive absorption ("punching a wall") to see whether aggressive orders are actually moving price or getting trapped (0:40).
3. The Proprietary "Nasdaq Effort" Model (Execution & Control): Measures multiple microstructure forces—imbalance, absorption, aggression, and path of least resistance—to confirm which side has control and where the order book is thin enough for price to explode (0:22).
Fabio emphasizes that he only filters for setups where all confirmations align in the same direction (e.g., value migration shifting higher plus the effort area showing upward pressure skew) (0:56).

2. Chapter 1 (0:00 – 7:26): "The Cage" — How Fabio Reads Who Dominates a Choppy Day

Brandon asks Fabio to walk through a recent consolidation day (Friday) and explain how order flow helps define bias and avoid getting chopped up (1:58).
• The Problem on Consolidation Days: Retail traders struggle to identify the relevant swing points, cannot tell when an impulse leg is finished, and get confused about when to buy the second leg (2:16).
• Defining "The Cage": At the session open, price pushed up into a red cluster area where buyers tried to push higher but were heavily absorbed by passive sellers (2:44). Price then dropped down and hit strong passive absorption from buyers at the bottom (3:32). Fabio calls this balanced back-and-forth range "The Cage" (3:32).
• Reading the Volume Profile Inside The Cage: Looking at the session profile on the right of his chart, a balanced bell-shaped profile indicates that buyers and sellers are comfortable at fair value—neither side is in control (3:38). Fabio avoids trading inside balance and waits for a directional auction outside of value (3:52).
• VWAP & Seller Takeover: Once price broke below the Volume Weighted Average Price (VWAP)—which Fabio uses to see where institutional players reload positions—and collapsed through the lower absorption zone, pure logic dictated that sellers had overpowered the early buyers (4:07). That breakdown provided a clean move from Point A to Point B (4:35).
• Mean-Reverting vs. Continuation Setups Using Delta:
	• Mean-Reverting Setup: At the extremes of a range, Fabio looks for one side "punching a wall"—for example, heavy positive buy delta at the top of a swing that produces zero upward price progress (a negative/red candle despite aggressive buying) (6:12).
	• Reload/Retest Setup: When buyers absorb sellers at a low and push price up, Fabio notes that catching the exact bottom spike is often too fast (6:59). Instead, he waits for price to pull back and retest that absorption zone (near the Value Area Low), sees sellers fail to make progress again, and enters on the confirmation (5:18).
• Using Delta Candles for Risk Management: As soon as Fabio enters a trade and sees an opposing delta candle confirm that his side has taken control, he immediately moves his stop-loss to breakeven ("risk to zero") (6:45).

3. Chapter 2 (7:26 – 13:36): The "Nasdaq Effort" Model & Market By Order (MBO) Signatures

Fabio clears the extra indicators off his chart to focus purely on his proprietary Nasdaq Effort indicator, which took years of study to develop (7:25).
• How "Nasdaq Effort" Works: It calculates the relationship between contract volume (effort) and price movement (result) (7:49). If the same number of contracts suddenly moves price upward much more easily than before, it signals that the order book is thin on the upside (the path of least resistance), meaning the market can explode in that direction (7:49). Visually, it prints colored boxes/areas showing stacked buyer or seller control (8:32).
• MBO (Market By Order) Signatures: Fabio also tracks large individual order prints (e.g., 111, 167, 300, or 345 contracts) using MBO data to distinguish whether a big volume spike came from one single large institutional participant or many small retail traders (9:25).
	• Why this matters: If a single large buyer stepped in with 300 contracts at a specific price, Fabio knows that institutional player will likely defend and reload their position if price returns to that exact level (10:02).
	• Warning on Raw Data: Fabio stresses that order-flow data alone is not a magic button—buying an MBO data feed doesn't make someone a profitable trader any more than buying a spreadsheet makes someone a data analyst (10:35). You must combine the data with a structural bias (10:35).
• Swing Profile "Discount" & "Land of Nowhere": By pulling a volume profile from swing low to swing high, Fabio divides the move into balance ("the land of nowhere," where he refuses to trade) and discount zones (using the analogy of buying a new iPhone below retail price) (11:46). He looks to execute his order-flow confirmations specifically inside these discount/value-area-low zones where heavy absorption previously occurred (12:22).

4. Chapter 3 (13:36 – 24:17): "Machine Gun 1:1 Scalps" & Value Shifts on Every Candle

Fabio then reveals the more aggressive, high-frequency variation of his strategy designed to capture short-term drifts even on days that aren't massive one-way trends (13:32).
• Reading Candle-by-Candle Value Migration ("Order Track"): Instead of looking only at the whole day's profile, Fabio inspects the Value Area and Point of Control (POC) inside every single candle (13:41).
	• If each consecutive candle's value area and POC shift lower, sellers control the drift (14:13).
	• When a candle closes green and its value area shifts higher than the previous candle's value area, it signals a momentum flip to the upside (14:06).
• The Mechanical 1:1 "Machine Gun" Execution:
	• Entry: Enter immediately on the close of a candle that confirms value area migration in the direction of the effort/delta bias (14:20).
	• Stop-Loss: Placed just below the migrating Value Area Low (for longs) or above the Value Area High (for shorts) (15:15).
	• Take-Profit: A fixed 1:1 risk-to-reward target (14:28).
	• Why 1:1 instead of 1:5? Fabio explains that aiming for 1:5 forces you to sit through deep pullbacks and only pays off on massive trend days (14:47). By firing multiple rapid 1:1 trades ("machine gunning") on every candle where value continues migrating, he can extract 3 to 4 consecutive wins out of a modest intraday drift (14:40).
	• Stretching to 1:2: If he sees heavy passive absorption blocking the opposing side while a candle is developing, he will occasionally stretch his target to 1:2 to build equity faster (17:09).
• Pyramiding / Rolling Positions on Strong Trends: When a sustained directional drift occurs, Fabio doesn't just take static 1:1s—he trails his first position to breakeven beneath the latest aggression/absorption, then adds ("loads") a second and third position on subsequent extreme delta/value shifts (19:10). This allows him to stack 3 to 4 rolling positions using only his initial 1R risk, turning linear gains into exponential returns (19:39).
• Why Fabio Doesn't Fully Automate This Model: Although he is an algorithmic researcher, Fabio states he would not automate this specific scalping model (18:46). An algorithm blindly takes every signal and holds static stops, whereas discretionary oversight lets him spot mid-candle absorption to cut risk to zero early or pyramid into sustained drifts (18:51).
• Market Specificity (Nasdaq vs. ES vs. Gold): Fabio warns against blindly copying a strategy across assets (20:52). Even though Nasdaq (NQ) and S&P 500 (ES) are heavily correlated, their microstructures are completely different (20:40). He has models that print money on Nasdaq and lose on ES, and vice versa (20:40). Because he specifically tested this model on Nasdaq over 3 years of historical data plus 3 months of live/forward out-of-sample testing (~300 trades), he refuses to claim it works on other markets without separate validation (20:12).

5. Chapter 4 (24:17 – End): The Achilles Heel — Chop, Commissions & NO Partial Profits

In the final segment, Brandon probes the weaknesses and edge cases of the strategy (24:22).
• Weakness #1: Consolidation / Chop Regimes: Because this is fundamentally a trend/drift-following microstructure model, choppy sideways markets are its biggest Achilles heel (22:05). In extreme chop, the win rate drops to 40%–50%, which results in a slight net loss after commissions on a 1:1 payoff (19:51). Fabio compares this to owning a tourism business in the UAE: you accept that summer is slow and focus on protecting capital until the season (volatility) returns (24:38).
• Weakness #2: Commission Drag on High-Frequency Scalping: Short-term 1:1 trading incurs heavy commission costs (19:58). Fabio notes that you can only overcome commission drag by strictly filtering for high-confluence setups where value migration, delta, and effort all point in the same direction (0:56).
• Portfolio Diversification Over "One Holy Grail": Fabio states that after countless backtests, no single model works in every market environment (28:08). To survive prop firm drawdown rules, he runs multiple uncorrelated models—pairing this intraday Nasdaq drift model with a longer-term mean-reverting model and a crypto model so that when one regime struggles, another carries the equity curve (27:45).
• Trailing Stop Rules & Why He Never Takes Partial Profits:
	• Trailing Mechanism: On his broader Nasdaq Effort model, Fabio trails his stop-loss using the printed Nasdaq Effort boxes (30:44). Once the market prints a second effort box in his favor, he moves his stop to breakeven; as a third and fourth box print, he locks in profit underneath each preceding box until the market reverses and stops him out (30:51).
	• No Partial Profits: Both Fabio and Brandon confirm from extensive algorithmic backtesting that taking partial profits hurts long-term expectancy (31:27). When you are wrong, you still take a 100% full stop-loss; when you are right on an asset like Nasdaq that can rally 200 points in a day, scaling out early caps your outliers that are mathematically required to pay for your losing streaks (31:27). (Fabio notes he does take partials on forex/currencies because currencies are far more mean-reverting, whereas Nasdaq has a strong directional skew) (31:49).
